Is there a natural approach to get explainability from polyfun. i.e. suppose I run finemapping twice: once with purse SUSIE, and once with SUSIE-polyfun. Suppose the PIP is diffuse in pure SUSIE, but more concentrated in SUSIE-polyfun. Is there a natural way to see the most important annotations to which to attribute this change?

Yes. There is a fairly natural explainability framework for this, although PolyFun does not provide it directly as a turnkey analysis.

The key is to separate two questions:

1. **How much did the PolyFun prior favor this SNP over its competitors?**
2. **Which annotations caused PolyFun to give it that favorable prior?**

Those can be analyzed separately.

### 1. First quantify the “prior lift” from PolyFun

Ordinary SuSiE uses uniform prior inclusion weights by default, whereas PolyFun supplies `SNPVAR`, proportional to its estimated per-SNP heritability, as the SuSiE `prior_weights`. ([Stephens Lab][1])

For SNP (i), define

[
L_i = \log\frac{\pi_i^{PF}}{\pi_i^{uniform}}
= \log(m\pi_i^{PF}),
]

where (m) is the number of variants in the locus.

So:

* (L_i=0): PolyFun did not favor the SNP relative to uniform.
* (L_i=2): its prior probability was (e^2\approx7.4) times higher than uniform.
* (L_i=-2): PolyFun penalized it about 7-fold.

Even better, for two competing variants (i,j),

[
\Delta^{prior}_{ij}
===================

\log\frac{\pi_i^{PF}}{\pi_j^{PF}}.
]

That quantity has a particularly clean interpretation. In a single-effect Bayesian regression,

[
\text{posterior odds}_{ij}
==========================

\text{BF}*{ij}\times
\text{prior odds}*{ij}.
]

SuSiE is a sum of such single-effect regressions, so although the multi-effect case is somewhat more complicated, this remains a good way to think about what PolyFun is doing. SuSiE explicitly treats `prior_weights` as the prior probability that each variable contains the effect. ([Stephens Lab][2])

Thus I would start any explainability report with something like:

| SNP | pure SuSiE PIP | PolyFun-SuSiE PIP | PolyFun prior | prior fold-vs-uniform |
| --- | -------------: | ----------------: | ------------: | --------------------: |
| A   |            .31 |               .78 |          .060 |                   12× |
| B   |            .28 |               .09 |          .006 |                  1.2× |
| C   |            .24 |               .05 |          .003 |                  0.6× |

That immediately tells you whether the concentration is really being driven by functional information.

---

## 2. Attribute that prior lift to annotations

Upstream, PolyFun begins from the S-LDSC model

[
\widehat h_i^2
==============

\sum_c \widehat{\tau}*c a*{ic},
]

where (a_{ic}) is annotation (c) for SNP (i) and (\widehat\tau_c) is the estimated annotation coefficient. PolyFun uses this genome-wide annotation model to rank variants by expected per-SNP heritability. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][3])

This gives you a very natural first-order annotation attribution:

[
C_{ic}=\widehat{\tau}*c a*{ic}.
]

But I would **not** simply rank (C_{ic}), because the important question isn't “why does SNP (i) have a large prior?” It is:

> Why does SNP (i) have a larger prior than the other statistically plausible SNPs in this locus?

So I would use a **local annotation contrast**.

Suppose (S) is the pure-SuSiE credible set. Define a weighted annotation average among the competitors,

[
\bar a_c
========

\frac{\sum_{j\in S,j\neq i}
PIP^{SuSiE}*j a*{jc}}
{\sum_{j\in S,j\neq i}PIP^{SuSiE}_j}.
]

Then define

[
\boxed{
C_c(i)
======

\widehat{\tau}*c
\left(a*{ic}-\bar a_c\right)
}
]

This is an especially useful explainability quantity.

It says:

> Relative to the SNPs that the genetic evidence considered plausible, how much did annotation (c) make SNP (i) look more functionally important?

For example:

| Annotation         | SNP A | credible-set average | (\hat\tau_c) | local contribution |
| ------------------ | ----: | -------------------: | -----------: | -----------------: |
| Coding             |     1 |                 0.03 |      large + |                +++ |
| Conserved          |  0.91 |                 0.32 |            + |                 ++ |
| Enhancer           |     1 |                 0.62 |            + |                  + |
| High-LD annotation |   0.1 |                  0.7 |            − |                  + |
| MAF bin            |     … |                    … |            … |                 ~0 |

This is much more informative than simply saying “SNP A is annotated as coding.”

---

## 3. There is an important wrinkle in PolyFun: the 20-bin step

This is the part I would keep in mind if you want rigorous attribution.

In the published PolyFun procedure, the annotation model does **not** directly determine the final prior weight. Instead:

[
\text{annotations}
\rightarrow
\sum_c\hat\tau_c a_{ic}
\rightarrow
\text{rank SNPs}
\rightarrow
\text{heritability bins}
\rightarrow
\text{re-estimated bin }h^2
\rightarrow
\text{SNPVAR}
\rightarrow
\text{SuSiE prior}.
]

Specifically, the original procedure partitions SNPs into 20 groups with similar annotation-predicted per-SNP heritabilities and then independently re-estimates heritability for those bins; the final prior is proportional to that re-estimated bin heritability. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][3])

This is done deliberately to make PolyFun robust to misspecification of the annotation model.

It means that

[
\hat\tau_c a_{ic}
]

is an **upstream explanation of why the SNP was ranked highly**, but is not literally an additive decomposition of its final `SNPVAR`.

In fact, two SNPs in the same final PolyFun bin generally receive the same functional prior. That gives you a very useful diagnostic:

> If two variants are in the same PolyFun bin, PolyFun is not directly using their annotation differences to distinguish between them.

If their PIPs nevertheless change differently between SuSiE and PolyFun-SuSiE, that can arise indirectly because changing priors on other variants changes the multi-effect SuSiE fit.

---

# 4. For rigorous explainability, I would use annotation ablation

This is probably the best answer if you want to publish an analysis saying:

> “The PolyFun-induced concentration of the credible set was primarily attributable to annotations X, Y and Z.”

Take annotation (c), remove its information, recompute PolyFun priors, and rerun exactly the same SuSiE model.

For example, for a focal SNP:

[
I_c =
PIP_i^{full}
------------

PIP_i^{-c}.
]

Or, if your phenomenon of interest is credible-set concentration, define a locus-level metric such as entropy:

[
H=-\sum_i PIP_i\log PIP_i
]

and compute

[
I_c =
H^{-c}-H^{full}.
]

Positive (I_c) says annotation (c) helped PolyFun concentrate the posterior.

Other good endpoints are:

[
I_c =
|CS_{95%}^{-c}|-|CS_{95%}^{full}|
]

or

[
I_c =
\max_i PIP_i^{full}-\max_i PIP_i^{-c}.
]

These answer slightly different questions.

### Two kinds of ablation are possible

**Fixed-model ablation** is the simpler one. Keep the fitted genome-wide (\hat\tau_c)'s but zero/remove annotation (c), recompute the SNP scores/bins/prior, and rerun SuSiE.

This answers:

> Given the PolyFun model that was learned, how much did this annotation contribute to this particular locus?

That is probably the explanation you want.

**Retrained ablation** reruns the genome-wide PolyFun/S-LDSC estimation with annotation (c) absent.

That answers:

> How much unique predictive information does this annotation contribute, allowing all the other annotations to compensate for its absence?

The two can differ substantially.

---

# 5. I would ablate annotation groups rather than 187 individual annotations

This is important because baseline-LF has many strongly related annotations: annotations and their windows, MAF categories, regulatory annotations, conserved regions, LD-related annotations, etc. The PolyFun paper's baseline-LF 2.2.UKB model contains 187 annotations. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][3])

Consequently, saying

> “H3K27ac contributed 37% and enhancer contributed 23%”

would imply much more identifiability than the model really has.

A better first analysis would group them into classes such as:

* coding/protein-altering
* promoter/TSS
* enhancer/regulatory
* chromatin marks/accessibility
* conserved/constrained
* MAF
* LD-related
* recombination/allele-age
* background-selection/diversity
* other sequence annotations

and perform **leave-one-group-out PolyFun-SuSiE**.

After finding that, say, “conservation” is important, you can drill down into its constituent annotations.

---

## 6. If you really want a single annotation-importance decomposition, Shapley values are natural

For a locus-level function such as

[
f(A)=PIP_i
]

or

[
f(A)=-H(PIP),
]

where (A) is the set of annotation groups included, compute Shapley values

[
\phi_c
======

E_S\left[
f(S\cup{c})-f(S)
\right].
]

This handles the fact that annotations interact and are correlated much more gracefully than single leave-one-out analyses.

I would do this over perhaps **8–15 annotation families**, not all 187 annotations. Exact Shapley values over 187 features would be both computationally absurd and scientifically hard to interpret; Monte Carlo Shapley over broad families would be quite feasible.

# 7. A particularly useful visualization

For each locus where PolyFun dramatically improves fine-mapping, I would make a plot with:

**Top panel:** pure SuSiE PIPs vs PolyFun-SuSiE PIPs.

**Second panel:** PolyFun prior fold-enrichment

[
m\pi_i^{PF}.
]

**Third panel:** the major annotation-group contributions

[
\widehat\tau_c(a_{ic}-\bar a_c).
]

Then perhaps a small ablation table:

| Removed group | target PIP | max PIP | 95% CS size |
| ------------- | ---------: | ------: | ----------: |
| none          |        .84 |     .84 |           3 |
| coding        |        .82 |     .82 |           3 |
| conservation  |        .51 |     .51 |          12 |
| regulatory    |        .68 |     .68 |           7 |
| LD/MAF        |        .79 |     .79 |           4 |

That would give a remarkably intuitive explanation:

> “SuSiE alone could not distinguish these 15 highly correlated variants. PolyFun assigned the lead variant an 11× larger prior than most competitors. Most of that prioritization arose because its conservation and regulatory annotations placed it in a high-heritable PolyFun stratum. Removing conservation reduced its PIP from 0.84 to 0.51.”

That is substantially more informative than simply listing annotations attached to the high-PIP SNP.

PolyFun itself provides `extract_annotations.py`, which extracts annotations carried by high-PIP SNPs, but this is essentially descriptive—it does not determine which annotations were responsible for the PIP increase. ([GitHub Wiki See][4])

### What I would implement

If I were adding an “explain PolyFun” function to a fine-mapping pipeline, I would use **three levels**:

1. **Prior lift:** (\log(m\pi_i)), exactly quantifying how strongly PolyFun favored each SNP.
2. **Local annotation contrast:** (\hat\tau_c(a_{ic}-\bar a_c)), giving a cheap, easily interpretable decomposition of why the annotation model favored it over its credible-set competitors.
3. **Group ablation:** rerun PolyFun-SuSiE after removing the top annotation families, to verify that those annotations actually account for the PIP concentration.

I think **#2 + #3 together** would make a quite defensible explainability method: #2 explains the mechanism, while #3 tests the counterfactual. It could probably be implemented fairly lightly on top of existing PolyFun outputs.

Suppose I am using what the polyfun wiki calls “approach 1”, in which I use a prior precomputed by the polyfun authors from uk biobank traits. To make use of your second suggestion, I need \hat\tau_c uk biobank annotation coefficients to go with this precomputed prior. Do the polyfun authors publish these annotation coefficients anywhere?

I checked this, and the answer is **not quite**: I do not find a release of the exact (\hat\tau_c) coefficients underlying the **Approach-1 precomputed prior**. There is, however, a closely related set of meta-analyzed annotation coefficients in the PolyFun paper's supplementary material.

The distinction matters.

The Approach-1 wiki says that the distributed prior consists of **meta-analyzed per-SNP heritabilities from 15 UK Biobank traits**. The repository ships these directly as `snpvar_meta.chr1_7.parquet` and `snpvar_meta.chr8_22.parquet`; `extract_snpvar.py` reads their `snpvar_bin` column and turns that directly into `SNPVAR`. There is no corresponding set of annotation coefficients shipped with those files. ([GitHub][1])

By contrast, when you actually run PolyFun's L2 S-LDSC stage yourself, the code explicitly writes files containing

[
\widehat\tau_c
]

with columns `ANNOTATION` and `ANNOTATION_COEFFICIENT`. So the software clearly has such coefficients internally for a trait-specific run; they simply are not part of the Approach-1 distribution. ([GitHub][2])

There is a potentially useful related resource: **Supplementary Table 3 of Weissbrod et al. 2020**. The paper states that it reports (\tau_c) estimates from a fixed-effects meta-analysis of **16 well-powered, genetically uncorrelated UK Biobank traits**, scaled to a common total heritability. These were used to define the functional architecture in their main simulations. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][3])

But I would **not treat those Supplementary Table 3 coefficients as the coefficients that generated Approach 1**.

The reason is more fundamental than the 16-versus-15-trait discrepancy. Approach 1 is based on the **final PolyFun per-SNP heritabilities**, after PolyFun's nonparametric procedure:

[
a_i
\overset{\hat\tau}{\longrightarrow}
\widehat h^2_{i,\mathrm{linear}}
\longrightarrow
\text{heritability bin}
\longrightarrow
\widehat h^2_{\mathrm{bin}}
\longrightarrow
\text{SNPVAR}_i.
]

PolyFun explicitly re-estimates the heritability of the bins after using the annotation coefficients to rank SNPs. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][3]) Thus the final prior distributed in Approach 1 is not generally expressible as

[
\mathrm{SNPVAR}*i=\sum_c \tau_c a*{ic}.
]

And then those final per-SNP quantities are meta-analyzed across traits. The repository even calls them “meta-analyzed per-SNP-h2 files.” ([GitHub][4])

So there may literally be **no single vector of (\tau_c)'s whose linear combination reproduces the Approach-1 prior**.

### What I would do instead

For explaining an Approach-1 fine-mapping result, I think the cleanest solution is to build a **surrogate model for the published prior**.

You have all the necessary public ingredients:

[
{a_{ic}}_{187\ \mathrm{annotations}}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\mathrm{SNPVAR}^{Approach1}_i
]

for millions of UKB variants. The baseline-LF 2.2.UKB annotation files themselves are publicly released. ([GitHub Wiki][5])

Fit, genome-wide, something like

[
\log(\mathrm{SNPVAR}_i)
=======================

\alpha+\sum_c \gamma_c a_{ic}+\epsilon_i
]

with ridge regression. Then at your locus use

[
C_c(i,j)
========

\gamma_c(a_{ic}-a_{jc})
]

to explain why the Approach-1 prior favors SNP (i) over SNP (j).

This has a nice property: the surrogate is trained to explain **the actual prior you used**, rather than an intermediate PolyFun model from a somewhat different collection of traits.

I would probably go one step further and fit two surrogates:

1. **Ridge linear model**, because it gives the simple additive annotation decomposition we discussed.
2. **A nonlinear model** such as gradient-boosted trees, with grouped SHAP values, as a check on how much of the Approach-1 prior cannot be captured by the linear annotation score.

If ridge achieves very high out-of-sample (R^2) for (\log(\mathrm{SNPVAR})), the (\gamma_c) decomposition becomes a very compelling approximation. If it does not, that itself tells you that the binning/meta-analysis step materially changed the relationship between annotations and the final prior.

One other possibility is to use the **16-trait (\tau_c)'s in Supplementary Table 3** as a simpler “biological attribution score.” I think that is reasonable for exploratory visualization, but I would label it explicitly as attribution to a *related UKB meta-analyzed baseline-LF model*, not an exact decomposition of the Approach-1 prior.

So, in short: **I don't think the exact Approach-1 (\tau_c)'s are published, and because of PolyFun's binning/re-estimation procedure, an exact single set may not exist.** But the public SNPVAR + 187 annotations make it quite feasible to reverse-engineer a very useful explanatory surrogate—arguably a better tool for your particular question.

Why do you have log(SNPVAR) in your proposed ridge regression?  In the original S-LDSC paper, SNPVar is a linear function of annotations.


You are right. For the purpose I proposed, **using (\log(\mathrm{SNPVAR})) was not the natural first choice**.

In S-LDSC, the model is explicitly

[
\operatorname{Var}(\beta_i)
===========================

# \sigma_i^2

\sum_c a_{ic}\tau_c,
]

so per-SNP heritability is linear in the annotations. ([Nature][1])

Therefore, if the goal is to construct a surrogate set of coefficients that can be interpreted analogously to S-LDSC (\tau_c)'s, I would instead fit

[
\boxed{
\mathrm{SNPVAR}^{A1}*i
\approx
\alpha+\sum_c \gamma_c a*{ic}
}
]

with ridge regularization.

Then the annotation-level explanation is directly additive:

[
\widehat{\mathrm{SNPVAR}}_i
===========================

\alpha+\sum_c \gamma_c a_{ic},
]

and for comparing two competing SNPs (i) and (j),

[
\widehat{\mathrm{SNPVAR}}_i-
\widehat{\mathrm{SNPVAR}}_j
===========================

\sum_c
\gamma_c(a_{ic}-a_{jc}).
]

That gives exactly the decomposition we want:

[
C_c(i,j)=\gamma_c(a_{ic}-a_{jc}).
]

### Why I initially suggested the log

I was thinking of it as a **generic predictive surrogate** for a positive quantity that may span a substantial dynamic range. A log transform can sometimes make such a prediction problem easier and prevents a few high-SNPVAR variants from dominating squared-error loss.

But that comes at a significant interpretability cost here. If

[
\log \mathrm{SNPVAR}_i
======================

\alpha+\sum_c\gamma_c a_{ic},
]

then

[
\mathrm{SNPVAR}_i
=================

e^\alpha\prod_c e^{\gamma_c a_{ic}},
]

so annotations combine **multiplicatively**, not additively. Your (\gamma_c)'s would then be contributions to *log prior*, rather than S-LDSC-like contributions to per-SNP heritability.

That is a different model from PolyFun's underlying S-LDSC model.

### There is still one wrinkle

The Approach-1 `SNPVAR` values are not simply the original S-LDSC linear predictions. PolyFun uses the linear annotation model to rank SNPs, assigns SNPs to bins, and then estimates per-SNP heritability separately for those bins. The final fine-mapping prior is proportional to this bin-level estimate. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][2])

Schematically,

[
a_i
\rightarrow
\sum_c\tau_c a_{ic}
\rightarrow
\text{rank/bin}
\rightarrow
\widehat{\sigma}_{b(i)}^2
\rightarrow
\mathrm{SNPVAR}_i.
]

So neither linear regression nor log-linear regression will exactly invert Approach 1.

But **linear regression is much more conceptually appropriate** if our objective is to reconstruct something resembling the missing annotation coefficients.

I would actually try three models and inspect them:

1. **Linear ridge**
   [
   y_i=\mathrm{SNPVAR}_i
   ]
   This is the primary model and gives S-LDSC-like additive attribution.

2. **Linear ridge predicting the rank or pre-bin ordering of SNPVAR.**
   Since PolyFun's original annotation model is primarily used to *rank* SNPs before binning, this may recover the latent annotation score particularly well.

3. **Log-SNPVAR ridge**, only as a predictive sensitivity analysis. If it predicts much better, that tells us something about the nonlinearity introduced by the binning/meta-analysis process, but I would not call its coefficients (\tau_c)-like.

There is also a subtle issue with positivity: ordinary S-LDSC (\tau_c)'s themselves can be negative, and the raw linear prediction can in principle behave badly. PolyFun uses regularization specifically to ensure sensible positive per-SNP heritabilities before its binning step. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][2]) So I would **not impose nonnegative coefficients** on the ridge regression; negative annotation coefficients are perfectly legitimate conditional effects.

So your correction changes my recommendation: **fit Approach-1 SNPVAR directly on the baseline-LF annotations, not log(SNPVAR), if the purpose is annotation attribution.** The resulting (\gamma_c)'s would not be the original UKB (\hat\tau_c)'s, but they would be a natural “effective (\tau)” decomposition of the published Approach-1 prior.
